Preliminaries: Limited Dependent Variables

When \(y\) Has Limited Values

  • We often have cases where \(y\) is dichotomous or comes in nominal/ordinal categories.

  • In these cases, OLS can be problematic:

    • OLS gives continuous predicted values that may be hard to interpret.

    • OLS can give predicted values that lie outside the range of the dependent variable.

    • The effect of any \(x_i\) on the outcome may not be linear across its range.

    • Heterodskedasticity is pretty much automatic.

Maximum Likelihood Approaches

  • For dichotomous data, we have probit/logit which use different probability distributions but produce similar results

  • For nominal data (i.e. unordered categories), we can use multinomial logit or probit.

  • For ordinal data, we can use ordered logit or probit.

  • In general: interpretation is the key. It is much trickier than OLS; the coefficients do not directly tell us the magnitude of change.

Example: Bias in Policy Professionals

  • Banuri et al. (2018) study whether policy professionals are subject to cognitive biases.

  • e.g. confirmation bias, when people remember or prefer information that conforms to prior views.

  • The survey sample consists of employees in the World Bank and DFID selected randomly and recruited via email.

  • The survey contained an experiment that randomized participants into one of two scenarios.

  • The outcome variable, measured in each scenario, is dichotomous.

The Two Scenarios

Participants see the same data but the scenario differs:

  • Control group: a non-ideological scenario involving a study about the effectiveness of a skin cream.

  • Treatment group: study about the effects for the bottom 40% of the income distribution when localities raise the minimum wage.

  • Are people in the treatment group more likely to interpret the data incorrectly?

  • Due to ideological predisposition, people in the treatment group might be more inclined to see what they want in the results.

Some Details of the Experiment

  • The same numbers were used in both cases, but the “Good outcome” and “Bad outcome” labels were randomized.

  • Above, the correct answer is that action Y is better, but this differs depending on the randomization.

  • The dependent variable is 1 if the person interprets the results correctly, and 0 otherwise.

Digging In: Dichotomous Dependent Variables

Dichotomous Data: Linear Probability Model

Actually, we could just use OLS despite a dichotomous \(y\).

\[\begin{align*} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[y_i|\mathbf{x_i}] & = [1 \times \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i = 1|\mathbf{x_i})] + [0 \times \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i = 0)|\mathbf{x_i}]\\ & = \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i = 1|\mathbf{x_i}) \end{align*}\]

We can rewrite the expected value of \(y_i\) given \(\mathbf{x_i}\) as:

\[\begin{equation*} \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i = 1|\mathbf{x_i}) = \mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta} \end{equation*}\]

Where \(\mathbf{x_i}\) is a \(1 \times k\) row vector from the matrix \(\mathbf X\), and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is a \(k \times 1\) column vector.

Example: Single Independent Variable

Interpretation

  • The interpretation is similar to OLS. Each one-unit increase in \(x\) leads to a \(\beta\)-sized change in the probability that \(y\) equals 1.

  • Problem: it’s unrealistic that \(x\) has the same, linear effect on \(\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i = 1)\) regardless of its starting level.

    • the estimated effect of \(x\) is biased for some values of \(x\).

  • Problem: predictions outside the boundaries of 0 and 1 are possible.

  • Problem: heteroskedasticity in the prediction errors. The errors are not normal, either.

Generalization to Multiple Independent Variables

\[\begin{align*} \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i = 1|\mathbf{x_i}) & = \mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta}\\ & = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{1} + \ldots + \beta_{k-1}x_{k-1} %+ \epsilon_i \end{align*}\]

  • Everything is the same. The inner product \(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is a scalar.

  • We can interpret each \(\beta_{k}\) as the predicted change in the probability that \(y_{i} = 1\) associated with a one-unit change in \(x_{k}\).

  • All the same problems are still present.

This is not optimal, but it’s not a huge problem either. We could just impose bounds on these extreme predictions.

In Defense of the LPM

  • The coefficients are easy to interpret and explain.

  • There are scenarios where a probit/logit cannot be estimated but an LPM can:

    • e.g. when an independent dummy variable is perfectly correlated with the outcome.

  • Standard errors in a logistic model can be too small.

The LPM Works Well in RCT Setup

  • In an RCT with a dichotomous outcome, the LPM provides a consistent estimator of the average treatment effect. \[\begin{equation*} y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}T + \beta_{2}x \end{equation*}\]

  • In the above, both \(y\) and \(T\) are dichotomous, and \(\beta_{1}\) is the estimated treatment effect.

  • Linearity doesn’t matter, since \(T\) has only two values. Also, \(x\) is uncorrelated with \(T\) due to random assignment.

  • In this setup, the marginal effect of \(T\) on average across the range of \(x\) is the same as in a probit/logit model.

An S-curve Offers Better Fit in Other Scenarios

Binary Response Models: Probit and Logit

  • These techniques use an S-shaped curve that is bounded between 0 and 1 to fit the data.

    • Probit: based on the cumulative normal distribution.

    • Logit: based on the cumulative logistic distribution.

  • In both, we think of the observed, dichotomous \(y\) as being generated by a latent, continuous variable \(y^{*}\).

    \[\begin{equation*} y_{i}=\begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } y^{*}_{i} > \tau \\ 0 & \text{if } y^{*}_{i} \leq \tau \end{cases} \end{equation*}\]

  • These are called link functions because they link \(y^{*}\) to \(y\).

Choosing the Distribution Function

\[\begin{equation*} y_{i}^{*} = \mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+ \epsilon_{i} \end{equation*}\]

  • There aren’t strong reasons to choose probit over logit or vice-versa. They yield equivalent results.

  • In both, we model \(y_{i}^{*}\) as a linear function of the \(x_i\)’s.

  • With probit, we assume standard normal errors: \(\epsilon_{i} \sim N(0,1)\)

  • With logit, we assume logistic errors: \(\epsilon_{i} \sim \lambda(0,\frac{\pi^2}{3})\)

    • Logit has slightly fatter tails due to the greater variance.

Probit Analysis

Probit: Cumulative Normal Density Function

\[\begin{equation*} \Phi(\epsilon) = \int_{-\infty}^{\epsilon} \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp \left(-\frac{(t - \mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}} \right) dt \end{equation*}\]
which, since we assume \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma =1\), simplifies to:

\[\begin{equation*} \Phi(\epsilon) = \int_{-\infty}^{\epsilon} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp \left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2} \right) dt \end{equation*}\]
Here we use the cumulative normal density to get the area under the normal distribution up to \(\epsilon\).

Normal Density vs. Cumulative Normal

Connection to Using a z-table

  • You should be familiar with looking up the area under the normal above/below some \(z\) score.

  • The cumulative standard normal density function gives the proportion of area below a \(z\) score.

  • This function evaluated at 0 is .5.

  • This function evaluated at 1.96 is .975.

Cumulative Normal Density Function

The Latent Variable \(y^{*}\)

\(y^{*}\) is a linear function of the observed \(x\)’s:

\[\begin{equation*} y_{i}^{*} = \mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+ \epsilon_{i} \end{equation*}\]

There is some threshold value of \(y^{*}\) above which \(y_{i}\) equals 1: \[\begin{equation*} y_{i}=\begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } y^{*}_{i} > \tau \\ 0 & \text{if } y^{*}_{i} \leq \tau \end{cases} \end{equation*}\]

We think of \(y_{i}^{*}\) as being determined by \(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) plus a stochastic component. The realization of \(y_i\) is thus probabilistic to some degree.

  • Since \(y_{i}^{*} = \mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+ \epsilon_{i}\), there is some probability that \(y_i^* < \tau\) once \(\epsilon_i\) is added on.

  • We can use the fact that \(\epsilon\) is assumed to have a standard normal distribution to find the area above/below the threshold.

Probit Analysis

We use the latent variable \(y^{*}\) and the cumulative normal density to calculate \(\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_{i} = 1 | \mathbf{x_{i}})\), setting \(\tau = 0\). \[\begin{align*} \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y = 1 | \mathbf x_{i}) & = \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y^{*} > 0 | \mathbf x_{i})\\[.2em] & = \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+ \epsilon > 0 | \mathbf x_{i})\\[.2em] & = \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(\epsilon > -\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}| \mathbf x_{i})\\[.2em] & = \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(\epsilon \leq \mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}| \mathbf x_{i})\\[.2em] & = \Phi(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \end{align*}\] The probability that \(y_{i}=1\) is given by the cumulative normal distribution evaluated at the value \(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

Illustration

Probit: Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Let \(p_{i}\) be the probability of observing the value of \(y_{i}\) we observed: \[\begin{equation*} p_{i}=\begin{cases} \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_{i}=1 | \mathbf{x_{i}}) & \text{if } y_{i}=1 \\ 1- \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_{i}=1 | \mathbf{x_{i}}) & \text{if } y_{i}=0 \end{cases} \end{equation*}\]

The likelihood function is thus: \[\begin{equation*} L(\boldsymbol{\beta}| \mathbf{y}, \mathbf{X}) = \prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i} \end{equation*}\]

We multiply together the respective probabilities of each observed \(y_{i}\)

Log Likelihood Function

\[\begin{align*} L(\boldsymbol{\beta}| \mathbf{y}, \mathbf{X}) & = \prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\\ & = \prod_{y=1}\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_{i} =1|\mathbf{x_{i}}) \prod_{y=0}[1 - \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_{i} =1|\mathbf{x_{i}})]\\ & = \prod_{y=1} \Phi(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \prod_{y=0}[1 - \Phi(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta})]\\ \end{align*}\]

Which makes the log likelihood function: \[\begin{equation*} \ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta}| \mathbf{y}, \mathbf{X}) = \sum_{y=1} \ln \Phi(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}) + \sum_{y=0}\ln[1 - \Phi(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta})] \end{equation*}\]

Estimation of a Probit

  • The MLE finds the values of the parameters \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) that maximize the given log likelihood.

  • We get a set of coefficients just like usual. The interpretation is different, however.

  • Stata: probit y x1 x2 x3

  • R: model <- glm(y ~ x1 + x2 + x3, family = binomial(link = "probit"), data = mydata)

Interpretation of Coefficients

  • The sign of the coefficient indicates the direction of the relationship between the \(x\)’s and \(\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i=1)\).

  • To understand the magnitude of the effect, we must determine by how much \(\Phi(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})\) changes in response to a particular \(x_i\).

  • This effect depends upon the values of all the variables in the analysis, since the starting point on the S-shaped curve matters.

  • There is no direct way to interpret the magnitude of the change in probability due to change in a single \(x_i\) without considering the values of the other \(x_i\)’s.

Different Ways to Interpret Probit Results

  • Predicted Probability. Calculate \(\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i=1)\) given the values of the \(x\)’s for a particular case of interest.

  • Change in Predicted Probability. Calculate the change in \(\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i=1)\) when \(x\)’s change from one set of values to another.

  • Marginal Effect. The predicted effect on \(\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i=1)\) from changing one \(x_{i}\), starting at specified values for the \(x\)’s.

  • Average Marginal Effect. The average effect on \(\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i=1)\) from changing one \(x_{i}\), calculated across all the cases in the sample.

    • Each case is evaluated at its own values for the \(x\)’s.

Calculating a Predicted Probability

The probability of a vote for Biden is \(\Phi(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})\). If PoliticalNews=2, Ideology=3, FauciTherm=65, BLMtherm=60, and BAplus=1 then:

\[\begin{align*} \mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta}& = -1.472 + .150(\text{PoliticalNews}) - .472(\text{Ideology})\\ & + .022(\text{FauciTherm}) + .023(\text{BLMtherm}) + .155(\text{BAplus})\\[.4em] & = -1.47 + .15(2) - .472(3) + .022(65) + .023(60) + .155(1)\\[.4em] & = .377 \end{align*}\]

To find the probability that a person with the above characteristics voted for Biden, evaluate using the cumulative normal distribution: \(\Phi(.377) = .65\).

Finding the Effect of \(\Delta x_k\)

  • Let the initial predicted value be \(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

  • Then, by changing \(x_k\) to some new value, we can calculate \(\mathbf{\tilde{x}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

  • The predicted effect of the change in \(x_k\) on \(\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i = 1)\) is given by: \[\begin{equation*} \Phi(\mathbf{\tilde{x}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}) - \Phi(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \end{equation*}\]

  • The values of all the variables in the analysis are relevant.

Example

Previously we found that the probability of a Biden vote is .65. What if we change Ideology from 3 to 5?

\[\begin{align*} \mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta}& = -1.472 + .150(\text{PoliticalNews}) - .472(\text{Ideology})\\ & + .022(\text{FauciTherm}) + .023(\text{BLMtherm}) + .155(\text{BAplus})\\[.1em] & = -1.47 + .15(2) - .472(5) + .022(65) + .023(60) + .155(1)\\[.1em] & = -.567 \end{align*}\]

Since \(\Phi(-.567) = .29\), we can calculate the effect of the change in Ideology like this: \[\begin{equation*} \Phi(-.567) - \Phi(.377)= .29 - .65 \ = \ -.36 \end{equation*}\] The probability of a Biden vote falls by .36 to .29.

Marginal Effect of \(x_k\)

As with OLS, we can find the marginal effect of \(x_k\) by taking the derivative with respect to \(x_k\). \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y=1|\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_k} = \frac{\partial \Phi(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial x_k} = \phi(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})\beta_k \end{equation*}\] where \(\phi(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})\) is the normal probability density function (note: this is not the cumulative density function!).

  • For the example case when Ideology=3, \(\phi(.377)=.372\).

  • Recall that the coefficient on Ideology is -.472.

  • Since \((.372)(-.472) = -.176\), the marginal effect of a one-unit change in Ideology is about -.18 for \(x\)’s set at these values.

Measures of Fit

  • In OLS, typical measures of the model’s fit include the \(F\)-statistic and the \(R^2\) statistic.

  • For probit analysis, the correlate to the \(F\)-test is a likelihood ratio (LR) test. The idea is the same: the null hypothesis is that all coefficients are zero.

  • The “pseudo-\(R^2\)” measure should be treated with caution. It’s not that useful a statistic.

  • What would be better? The percentage of cases predicted correctly. Use the estat command.

The Likelihood Ratio (LR) Test

  • The test involves a comparison of the likelihood of the estimated model to the likelihood of a restricted model.

  • In the restricted model, the coefficients on the \(x_i\)’s are restricted to 0. This is an intercept-only model (Iteration 0 in the MLE).

  • A statistically significant increase in the likelihood in the unrestricted model means that the \(x_i\)’s have explanatory power. \[\begin{equation*} LR = -2 \ln\left(\frac{L_r}{L_{ur}}\right) = -2(\ln L_r - \ln L_{ur}) \end{equation*}\]

  • The LR statistic has a \(\chi^2\) distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of restricted coefficients.

\(LR = -2(\ln L_r - \ln L_{ur}) = -2(-3469.50 - (-1173.74)) = 4591.52\)

Pseudo-\(R^{2}\) is not a great measure of fit. Avoid it.

LR Test for Joint Significance

  • We can also use an LR test to determine whether two or more coefficients are jointly significant.

  • For the restricted model, obtain the final log likelihood of the model estimated without including these \(x_i\)’s.

  • For the unrestricted model, use the final log likelihood of the full model. \[\begin{equation*} LR = -2(\ln L_r - \ln L_{ur}) \end{equation*}\]

  • The LR statistic has a \(\chi^2\) distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of restricted coefficients.

Logit Analysis

Logit Analysis

  • The setup is very similar to that of Probit analysis, but it uses the cumulative standard logistic distribution. \[\begin{equation*} \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i = 1|\mathbf{x_{i}}) = \Lambda (\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta})} \end{equation*}\]

  • We assume \(\epsilon_i\) has a logistic distribution with mean 0 and variance \(\pi^{2}/3\).

  • \(\Lambda (\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta})\) gives the area up to the point \(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) in the cumulative standard logistic distribution.

Normal vs. Logistic Densities

Cumulative Normal vs. Cumulative Logistic

Basics of Logit

  • An odds ratio is the probability of an outcome divided by its complement (i.e. the probability that it will not happen).

  • Logit uses the log of odds ratio for modeling \(y^*\) \[\begin{equation*} \ln \left(\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}\right) = \mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+ \epsilon_{i} \end{equation*}\]

  • The odds ratio is: \(\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}\). Taking the natural log makes it linear.

  • The coefficients thus give us changes in the log odds ratio. We can convert this into changes into predicted probabilities.

Math Note

Given that: \[\begin{align*} \mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+ \epsilon_{i} & = \ln \left(\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}\right)\\ \exp(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+ \epsilon_{i}) & = \frac{p_i}{1-p_i} \end{align*}\] Then, \[\begin{align*} \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta})} & = \frac{\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}}{1 + \frac{p_i}{1-p_i}} \ = \ \frac{\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}}{\frac{1-p_{i}}{1-p_{i}} + \frac{p_i}{1-\hat{p}_i}} \ = \ \frac{\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}}{\frac{1}{1-p_i}} \ = \ p_{i}\\ \end{align*}\]

Estimating Logit Models

  • Stata: logit y x1 x2 x3

  • R: model <- glm(y ~ x1 + x2 + x3, family = binomial(link = "logit"), data = mydata)

  • R: the function plogis(w) gives the cumulative logistic distribution at point \(w\).

Example Interpretation (Logit)

The probability of a vote for Biden is \(\Lambda(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})\). If PoliticalNews=2, Ideology=3, FauciTherm=65, BLMtherm=60, and BAplus=1: \[\begin{align*} \mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta}& = -2.691 + .295(\text{PoliticalNews}) - .898(\text{Ideology})\\ & + .041(\text{FauciTherm}) + .043(\text{BLMtherm}) + .282(\text{BAplus})\\[.4em] & = -2.269 + .295(2) - .898(3) + .041(65) + .043(60) + .281(1)\\[.4em] & = .733 \end{align*}\]

Predicted Probabilities in More Detail

Let \(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta}= \beta_{0} + \beta_{1} x_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{2}\) \[\begin{align*} \Lambda(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta}) & = \Lambda(\beta_{0} + \beta_{1} x_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{2})\\[.75em] & = \frac{\exp(\beta_{0} + \beta_{1} x_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{2})}{1 + \exp(\beta_{0} + \beta_{1} x_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{2})} \end{align*}\]

Finding the Effect of \(\Delta x_k\)

  • Quite similar to probit, the predicted effect of the change in \(x_k\) on \(\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y_i = 1)\) is given by: \[\begin{equation*} \Lambda(\mathbf{\tilde{x}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}) - \Lambda(\mathbf{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \end{equation*}\]

  • The values of all the variables in the analysis are relevant.

Example

Previously we found that the probability of a Biden vote is .68. What if we change Ideology from 3 to 5? \[\begin{align*} \mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta}& = -2.691 + .295(\text{PoliticalNews}) - .898(\text{Ideology})\\ & + .041(\text{FauciTherm}) + .043(\text{BLMtherm}) + .282(\text{BAplus})\\[.4em] & = -2.269 + .295(2) - .898(5) + .041(65) + .043(60) + .281(1)\\[.4em] & = -1.063 \end{align*}\] Since \(\Lambda(-1.063)\)= .26, we can calculate the effect of the specified change in Ideology: \[\begin{equation*} \Lambda(-1.063) - \Lambda(.733)= .26 - .68 \ = \ -.42 \end{equation*}\]

The probability of a Biden vote falls by .42 to .26.

Marginal Effect of \(x_k\)

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial \mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y=1|\mathbf{x_i})}{\partial x_k} = \frac{\partial \Lambda(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial x_k} = \lambda(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})\beta_k \end{equation*}\]
where \(\lambda(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})\) is the logistic probability density function. \[\begin{equation*} \lambda(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{[1 + \exp(\mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta})]^2} \end{equation*}\]

For the example case: when Ideology=3, \(\lambda(.733)=.219\).
Since \((.219)(-.898) = -.20\), the marginal effect of Ideology is -.20 when the \(x\)’s are set to the stated values.

Interpretation with Odds Ratios

The logit model can be written such that: \[\begin{equation*} \ln \Omega(x) = \mathbf{x_i}\boldsymbol{\beta} \end{equation*}\]
where \(\Omega(x)\) is the odds of an event given \(x\): \[\begin{equation*} \Omega(x) = \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y=1|x)}{\mathop{\mathrm{Pr}}(y=0|x)} \end{equation*}\]
\(\ln \Omega(x)\) is the thus the log of the odds ratio.

Interpretation with Odds Ratios

Since the model in linear in \(\beta_{k}\): \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial \ln \Omega(x)}{\partial x_{k}} = \beta_{k} \end{equation*}\]

  • A one-unit change in \(x_{k}\) is associated with a \(\beta_{k}\) change in the log odds ratio.

  • Thus, a one-unit change in \(x_{k}\) is associated with an \(\exp(\beta_{k})\) change in the odds ratio.

  • If \(\exp(\beta_{k}) >1\), the odds are \(\exp(\beta_{k})\) times larger. If \(\exp(\beta_{k}) <1\), the odds are \(\exp(\beta_{k})\) times smaller.

Logistic Regression

  • We can get the coefficients reported as the changes in the odds ratios using the logistic command, rather than logit.

  • In other words, where logit gives us \(\beta_{k}\), logistic will give us a coefficient that equals \(\exp(\beta_{k})\).

  • If we use the logistic command but add the option coef, we get the same coefficients as the logit command.

Logistic Regression with Odds Ratios

A one-unit increase in Ideology is associated with decreasing the odds of voting for Biden by a factor of .4073.

Key for Interpretation

  • Odds ratios can never be negative. Rather than be centered around 0, they are centered around 1.

  • Why? Because if a ratio is multiplied by 1, it does not change.

  • Thus, a variable has a negative effect on the odds of the outcome if the change in the ratio is less than 1.

  • A variable has a positive effect if the odds ratio changes by a factor greater than 1.

Example

  • Suppose the initial odds of a person voting for Biden are 7 to 3. That is an odds ratio of 2.333 and a probability of \(\frac{7}{7 + 3}\) or .70.

  • If Ideology increases by 1, the odds ratio would change by a factor of .4073, making it \((2.333)(.4073) = .9052\).

  • That’s odds of .9052 to 1, which is about .48 in probability terms: \[\begin{equation*} \frac{.9052}{.9052 + 1} \approx .48 \end{equation*}\]

  • Predicted probabilities are probably easier to interpret, but some disciplines (e.g. public health) tend to use odds ratios.

Advice

  • Within the same model, odds ratios do have some value in communicating relative risk for different groups.

  • Even in fields like health research, however, the use of odds ratios is under question (e.g. article by Norton et al.).

  • Odds ratios are not comparable across different models or studies.

  • Predicted probabilities and marginal effects are much more understandable to most audiences.

Concluding Points

  • With dichotomous dependent variables, both the LPM and probit/logit models have virtues.

  • Probit/logit models are better for unbiased predictions of marginal effects for specific hypothetical cases.

  • The LPM offers easier interpretation of coefficients.

  • When RCTs have a dichotomous DV, the LPM is just fine.
